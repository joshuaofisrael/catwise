Yes, most pet cats recognize their own name. In a 2019 study in Scientific Reports, household cats reacted more to their own name than to ordinary words of the same length and rhythm, and they still picked out their name when a stranger said it. A 2022 follow up found that cats living in homes also seem to learn the names of the other cats they live with. Recognizing a name is not the same as coming when called, though, and many cats show they heard you with nothing more than a flick of the ear.

The 2019 study: cats pick their name out of a list

Atsuko Saito and colleagues at the University of Tokyo and Sophia University ran four experiments in cats' own homes and in one cat café in Japan. They used a habituation and dishabituation test, which needs no training. A hidden speaker played five recordings, 15 seconds apart. The first four were words the cat had no reason to care about. The fifth was the cat's own name. If a cat stops reacting as the boring words go by, then perks up again at its name, it is telling the two apart.

The four "boring" words were chosen carefully. They were everyday Japanese nouns with the same number of syllable beats and the same accent pattern as the cat's name, and the speakers were asked to say them in the same tone of voice they used for the name. The team also checked the recordings acoustically to make sure the name did not simply stand out by being louder, longer or higher pitched.

  • Experiment 1 (owner's voice, nouns): of 16 household cats, 11 settled down over the four nouns, and 9 of those 11 reacted more strongly when they heard their name.
  • Experiment 2 (other cats' names): cats living with four or more other cats heard their housemates' names first. Household cats picked out their own name; the café cats did not.
  • Experiment 3 (multi cat homes, nouns): cats from busy households and from the café both told their name apart from ordinary nouns.
  • Experiment 4 (a stranger's voice): two women the cats had never met recorded the words and names. Cats still reacted more to their own name, so they were not just responding to their owner's voice.

The authors called this the first experimental evidence that cats can tell human words apart. Their explanation is learning: a cat hears its name far more than any other word, and that name tends to come right before food, play, petting, or sometimes a trip to the vet.

The 2022 study: cats know who their housemates are

Saho Takagi and colleagues at Kyoto University and Azabu University asked whether a cat links a name to a particular individual. Each cat heard its owner call the name of another cat in the household four times, then saw a photo of a cat's face on a laptop screen. Sometimes the face matched the name and sometimes it did not.

Household cats looked at the screen longer when the face did not match the name, a sign that the mismatch surprised them. Cats from cat cafés did not show this. In a second experiment with human family members' names and faces, the effect was not seen across all cats, but it grew stronger in larger families, and especially in cats who had lived with their family for longer. The authors note that time with the family was closely tied to the cat's age, so they could not separate the two.

The researchers did no training at all. The cats appear to have picked up these name and face links simply by living with people who use those names every day.

Why café cats did worse

In both studies, café cats did worse than cats in ordinary homes. In a café, many different visitors call many different names, in different voices, and the wrong cat often walks over and gets the treat anyway. The link between a name and what happens next is weaker. A house cat with one or two regular people gets a much clearer signal. The researchers caution that they tested only one café in the 2019 study, so this is a likely explanation rather than a proven one.

Why your cat might not come when called

Hearing and recognizing a name does not oblige a cat to do anything about it. In the 2019 experiments, more than half of the cats responded by moving their ears or head, and fewer than 10% meowed, moved their tail or moved from where they were. An earlier study by Saito and Shinozuka in 2013 saw the same pattern when cats heard their owner's voice: they clearly told it apart from strangers' voices, but mostly showed it with ear and head movements. So an ear swivel and no other movement is often a cat that heard you perfectly well.

Practical tips for cat owners

The studies did not test training methods, but their explanation, that a name matters because of what it predicts, gives some sensible pointers:

  1. Pair the name with good things. Say your cat's name just before meals, treats, play or a gentle chin scratch.
  2. Do not save the name for bad news. If the name mostly comes before the carrier or a pill, it predicts something the cat wants to avoid.
  3. In a multi cat home, call one cat at a time when you want a specific cat, and reward the cat you called. The 2019 authors suggest that hearing only its own name called helps a cat learn which name is its own.

Cat names FAQ

Do cats really know their names?

Most pet cats recognize their own name. A 2019 study in Scientific Reports found household cats reacted more to their name than to similar sounding everyday words, even when a stranger said it.

Why doesn't my cat come when I call its name?

Recognizing a name is not the same as choosing to respond. In the research, most cats showed they heard their name by moving their ears or head, and fewer than 10% meowed, moved their tail or moved from where they were.

Do cats know the names of other cats?

Probably, if they live together in a home. A 2022 study found household cats expected to see a specific housemate's face after hearing that cat's name, though cats from cat cafés did not.

How do I teach my cat its name?

Researchers think cats learn names because of what follows them. Say the name right before food, treats or play, and avoid using it only before things your cat dislikes.
