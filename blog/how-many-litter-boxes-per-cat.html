How many litter boxes do I need per cat?

By the CatWise team · Last updated 8 October 2026

Short answer: one litter box per cat, plus one extra. One cat needs two boxes, two cats need three, three cats need four. Just as important, the boxes should be in different quiet places, not lined up side by side, with at least one on every floor of your home. This "N+1" rule of thumb appears in both the AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines and the AAFP/ISFM house soiling guidelines.

Why one box per cat is not enough

Cats are fussy about where they toilet for good reasons. In the wild, a toileting spot that feels exposed, dirty or contested is a risk. At home, the same instincts mean a cat may avoid a box that another cat is guarding, that smells too strongly of other cats, or that sits next to something frightening like a noisy washing machine. The extra box gives every cat a choice, which is one of the core principles of the AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines: cats should have more than one option for each key resource, spread out so that no cat has to run a gauntlet to reach it.

The guidelines also make a point that surprises many owners: cats that eat or sleep near each other do not necessarily get along. Many cats in multi cat homes quietly "time share" resources, using them at different times to avoid each other. If your cats are not genuinely friendly, the N+1 rule becomes a minimum rather than a target.

Why location matters as much as number

Two boxes side by side count, from a cat's point of view, as one toileting area. The house soiling guidelines recommend:

  • Separate locations, so one cat cannot block access to every box at once.
  • At least one box on each level of a multi story home.
  • Quiet, low traffic spots with an escape route, away from appliances that start suddenly.
  • Away from food and water. Cats prefer not to eat near where they toilet.

What size litter box?

Bigger than you think. The AAHA/AAFP guidelines recommend a box at least one and a half times the length of the cat from nose to tip of the tail, and point out that this means most manufactured boxes are too small. A large, low sided plastic storage box is a practical option. For kittens and older cats with stiff joints, make sure at least one box has a low entry; International Cat Care notes that cats with arthritis may toilet outside a box that is painful to climb into.

Which litter?

Individual cats have documented preferences, so the best approach is to offer choices in separate boxes and let your cat decide. Many cats do well with an unscented, clumping litter, and the AAHA/AAFP guidelines note unscented clumping litter may be preferred for cats with a history of urinary problems. Avoid scented litters and air fresheners next to the box: a cat's sense of smell is far more sensitive than ours.

How often to clean

  • Scoop at least once a day. Some cats will avoid a dirty box altogether.
  • Wash and refill regularly, using hot water rather than soap or strong chemicals, which leave smells cats dislike.
  • Replace scratched boxes, since scratches hold odor.

Covered or open?

The guidelines note that whether a box is open or covered may matter to some cats. Covers trap odor inside, and a cat inside a hood cannot see another cat approaching. If you use covered boxes, offer at least one open box too and see which your cat chooses.

When the rule is not enough

The N+1 rule is a starting point. You may need more boxes if:

  • your cats live in separate social groups (count one box per group plus one, as the AAHA/AAFP guidelines suggest, and give each group its own litter station);
  • you live in a large or multi level home;
  • you have an older cat with arthritis or kidney disease who needs a box close to where they rest;
  • a cat is already toileting elsewhere, in which case adding a box where the accidents happen is often a useful first step.

When it is not about the box

Changes in litter box habits are very often medical. Urinary tract disease, arthritis, kidney disease and diabetes can all cause a cat to pee outside the box or visit it more often. Book a vet visit before reorganizing your home. If a cat, especially a male, is straining and producing little or no urine, treat it as an emergency: Cornell Feline Health Center describes urethral obstruction as an absolute emergency. Litter box FAQ

How many litter boxes do I need for two cats?

Three, in three different locations. The rule of thumb is one per cat plus one extra.

Does a single cat need two litter boxes?

Yes. The AAFP and ISFM house soiling guidelines recommend two litter boxes in two separate locations for a single cat.

Can I put two litter boxes next to each other?

It is better not to. Boxes side by side function as one toileting area, so one cat can block both. Place them in different parts of the home.

How big should a litter box be?

At least one and a half times your cat's length from nose to tail tip, according to the AAHA/AAFP guidelines, which is larger than most boxes sold.
