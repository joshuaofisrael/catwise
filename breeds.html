Cat breeds compared: coats, traits and inherited health risks

By the CatWise team · Last updated 8 October 2026

There is no single best cat breed, but there are big differences in coat care and, above all, in inherited health risks. Many pedigree breeds carry known genetic conditions, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM, a heart disease) or polycystic kidney disease (PKD), and several have DNA tests. Use the table below to see which conditions to ask a breeder about before you choose a kitten.

"Test listed for the breed" means the UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory considers the variant relevant to that breed, not that every cat of the breed has it. Breed lists change as research grows, so ask your vet or the lab for the latest panel.

What the main conditions are

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)

A thickening of the heart muscle. Cornell notes it is more prevalent in breeds including the Maine Coon, Ragdoll, British Shorthair, American Shorthair, Sphynx, Bengal, Chartreux, Norwegian Forest Cat, Siberian and Persian. DNA tests exist only for specific variants found in Maine Coons (A31P) and Ragdolls (R820W). A negative result for those variants does not mean a cat can never develop HCM, which is why some breeders also have their cats' hearts scanned.

Polycystic kidney disease (PKD1)

Cysts form in both kidneys, often before 12 months of age, and can lead to kidney failure later in life. It is inherited in a dominant way, so one copy of the variant is enough to cause disease. UC Davis lists it for Persians and Persian derived breeds and several others.

Pyruvate kinase (PK) deficiency

An inherited anemia that can come and go, with signs such as lethargy, weakness, weight loss and jaundice. It is recessive: carriers are healthy but can pass it on.

Progressive retinal atrophy (PRA)

Inherited degeneration of the retina. The rdAc form causes slowly progressive blindness in adult cats; the rare rdy form causes blindness in very young Abyssinian and Somali kittens.

Burmese hypokalemia

Episodes of low blood potassium that cause muscle weakness, most obvious in the neck, so affected cats may struggle to walk or hold their head up.

Scottish Fold osteochondrodysplasia

The same cartilage defect that folds the ears also affects bones and joints, leading to painful arthritis, a stiff gait, a thickened inflexible tail and difficulty jumping. There is no cure; vets manage the pain.

Questions to ask a breeder

  1. Which DNA tests have both parents had, and can I see the results? For a recessive condition such as PK deficiency, look for parents that are N/N (clear) or a carrier bred only to a clear cat.
  2. Have the parents had heart screening? Especially for breeds where HCM is more common. Ask what was done and when.
  3. Can I meet the kitten with its mother? Seeing the mother and the home gives clues to health and early socialization (see kitten care).
  4. What vaccinations, deworming and vet checks has the kitten had? Ask for written records.
  5. Does the breed standard favor an extreme feature? Very flat faces and folded ears come with known health costs.

Pedigree or mixed breed?

A pedigree gives you a more predictable coat and look, plus access to DNA testing for known breed conditions. A mixed breed cat from a shelter or rescue is not guaranteed to be healthier, but it is less likely to carry the specific variants concentrated in a single breed. Whichever you choose, routine checkups matter more than the label: see how often a cat should see the vet.

Cat breed FAQ

Which cat breeds are prone to heart disease (HCM)?

Cornell Feline Health Center lists HCM as more prevalent in the Maine Coon, Ragdoll, British Shorthair, American Shorthair, Sphynx, Bengal, Chartreux, Norwegian Forest Cat, Siberian and Persian. DNA tests exist for specific variants in Maine Coons and Ragdolls, but a negative test does not rule out HCM.

Are Scottish Fold cats healthy?

No. The mutation that folds the ears also affects cartilage and bone, causing painful joint disease (osteochondrodysplasia) of varying severity in folded cats. International Cat Care considers it unethical to keep breeding them.

What does a cat DNA test result of N/N mean?

N/N means the cat did not inherit the tested variant from either parent, so it will not develop that specific condition and cannot pass it on. For a recessive condition such as PK deficiency, one copy (for example N/K) means a healthy carrier. For a dominant condition such as PKD1, one copy (N/P) means the cat is affected.

Do mixed breed cats need genetic testing?

Not routinely. Regular veterinary checkups, including listening to the heart and checking kidney health as cats age, are the main safeguard for mixed breed cats.
