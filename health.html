Cat health: warning signs, vaccines, checkups and poisons

By the MeowWise team · Last updated 8 October 2026

Cats are good at hiding illness, so small changes matter. Go to a vet immediately if your cat has difficulty breathing, collapses, strains to pee and passes little or nothing, has eaten a lily or a human medicine, has a seizure, or is in obvious pain. For everything else, routine checkups (at least yearly for adults, every six months over age 10), core vaccines and parasite control prevent most problems.

Go to a vet now: emergency signs

Based on the emergency conditions listed by Cornell University Hospital for Animals and Cornell Feline Health Center guidance:

  • Difficulty breathing, or blue, purple or pale gums
  • Collapse, unresponsiveness, or sudden weakness or loss of balance
  • Straining in the litter box with little or no urine, especially in a male cat (why this cannot wait)
  • Eating any part of a lily, a human medicine, antifreeze, rodent poison or a dog flea product (lilies)
  • Seizures or tremors
  • Trauma such as a fall or a road accident, even if the cat seems fine
  • Not putting weight on a leg, or crying out in pain
  • Severe or repeated vomiting, or diarrhea with blood
  • A bloated, hard or painful belly

If you think your cat has been poisoned, call your vet or an animal poison control service straight away and take the packaging or plant with you.

Subtle signs worth a vet visit

These are not usually emergencies, but they are often the first sign of disease in cats:

  • Eating less, or stopping eating. Cornell warns that a cat that refuses food can develop serious medical problems, so do not wait it out.
  • Drinking or peeing more than usual (common in kidney disease, diabetes and an overactive thyroid)
  • Weight loss or gain, or a change in coat condition or grooming
  • Hiding more, sleeping in new places, or being reluctant to jump
  • Peeing or pooping outside the litter box
  • Bad breath, drooling or dropping food (signs of mouth pain)

Vaccines

The 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines treat these as core vaccines for pet cats:

  • Feline panleukopenia virus (FPV), feline herpesvirus 1 (FHV-1) and feline calicivirus (FCV), usually given together as one combination vaccine
  • Rabies, as required by local law
  • Feline leukemia virus (FeLV) for cats under one year old; for older cats it depends on lifestyle and risk

Kittens start the combination vaccine no earlier than 6 weeks of age, repeated every 3 to 4 weeks until 16 to 20 weeks, with a further dose at 6 months. After that, the FPV, FHV-1 and FCV vaccine is typically repeated every 3 years for adult pet cats, while other vaccines vary. Schedules differ between countries and products, so follow your vet's plan. See kitten vaccines.

Parasites

Fleas, ticks and worms are common, even in indoor cats. Use a cat specific product your vet recommends. Never use a dog flea treatment on a cat: many contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats and can cause twitching, seizures and death. International Cat Care runs a long standing campaign about this.

Common poisons in the home

Cornell lists these among the substances most often involved in cat poisoning calls to the ASPCA:

  • Human medicines: pain relievers such as acetaminophen (paracetamol), ibuprofen and aspirin, plus cold remedies and antidepressants
  • Insecticides, including flea products made for dogs
  • Plants: lilies above all, which can cause fatal kidney failure in tiny amounts, plus tulips, foxglove, philodendron and others
  • Human foods: onions, grapes, raisins, chocolate and gum with xylitol (see foods to avoid)
  • Household products: antifreeze, rodent poison, bleach and other cleaners

Common illnesses

  • Dental disease. Cornell reports studies finding 50 to 90% of cats over four have some form of dental disease. It can be painful enough to stop a cat eating.
  • Lower urinary tract disease. Straining, frequent small pees, blood in the urine or peeing outside the box. In male cats it can lead to a life threatening blockage.
  • Chronic kidney disease (CKD). According to Cornell, it affects up to 40% of cats over 10 and 80% of cats over 15, and early stages often show no signs, which is why senior blood and urine tests matter. See senior cats.
  • Obesity. Cornell calls it the most common nutrition related problem in cats; it raises the risk of arthritis and diabetes. See nutrition.
  • Heart disease (HCM). More common in some breeds. See breed health checklist.

Cats and human health

Toxoplasmosis is the infection people most often ask about. Cats shed the parasite in their feces, but the eggs take at least a day to become infectious, so scooping the litter box daily reduces the risk. Pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system should ideally have someone else clean the litter box, or wear gloves and wash their hands afterwards. Living with a cat does not need to end: see myths vs facts.

Cat health FAQ

How do I know if my cat is sick?

Look for changes from normal: eating or drinking more or less, weight change, hiding, less grooming, reluctance to jump, or changes in litter box habits. Cats hide illness well, so any lasting change is worth a vet visit.

What cat symptoms are an emergency?

Difficulty breathing, collapse, pale or blue gums, straining to pee with little or no urine, eating a lily or a human medicine, seizures, trauma, and severe vomiting or bloody diarrhea all need a vet immediately.

What vaccines does my cat need?

The AAHA/AAFP core vaccines are feline panleukopenia, feline herpesvirus 1 and feline calicivirus (one combination vaccine), rabies where required by law, and feline leukemia virus for cats under one year. Your vet will tailor the rest to your cat's lifestyle.

Can I give my cat paracetamol or ibuprofen?

No. Common human pain relievers, including acetaminophen (paracetamol), ibuprofen and aspirin, are a frequent cause of poisoning in cats. Only give medicines your vet has prescribed for your cat.
